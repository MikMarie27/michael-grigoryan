# Meeting Tigran Tsitoghdzyan — a preparation brief

For Michael Grigoryan, 24, painter. Meeting: tomorrow.

---

## 1. Who he is, in the part that matters to you

Tigran Tsitoghdzyan (b. 1976, Yerevan) is one of the most internationally
visible Armenian artists alive. He lives in New York, shows with Opera Gallery
and others, and is best known for the *Mirrors* series — very large black-and-
white hyperreal portraits with hands across the faces.

Three facts about him are worth more to you than his CV:

**He was you.** He was identified as a prodigy as a child, had his first solo
shows — local and international — at ten years old, and some of his childhood
paintings are in the Children's Art Museum in Yerevan. When he looks at a
24-year-old with an obsessive hand, he is not being charitable. He is
recognising something.

**He is deliberately building a pipeline for young Armenian artists.** He keeps
a studio and studio-gallery in Armenia alongside New York, hosts events in
Yerevan for young artists, has mentored for the Creative Armenia–AGBU
Fellowship, and has publicly described plans for an artist residency, a gallery
for emerging artists, and an international creative hub in Yerevan. Meeting
young artists is not a favour he is doing between other things. It is a thing
he is actively doing.

**He is himself moving away from realism.** He has said he wants to work more
freely, without models or references, and has been experimenting with a new
colour series. Your work is nothing like his — but it is entirely about colour
and invention, which is the direction his own attention is currently pointed.
That is a real conversational advantage. Do not be intimidated by the gap
between hyperrealism and what you do; it is the reason the conversation is
interesting to him.

## 2. Why he is interested in your work specifically

Be clear-eyed about this, because it tells you what to lean on.

- **It is unmistakably made by hand, slowly.** Every roof is counted tile by
  tile. In 2026 that is scarce and it is immediately legible to another artist.
  It cannot be faked and it cannot be generated.
- **It has a real, unusual formal idea** — the collapsed multi-viewpoint
  perspective, and skies rendered as flat ribbons rather than atmosphere. It
  isn't decorative; it is a consistent way of seeing.
- **There is an Armenian thread** — the street scene with Armenian lettering in
  the sky and blossom on the trees. Given what he is building in Yerevan, an
  Armenian artist making Armenian subjects in an original idiom is exactly the
  profile he is looking for.
- **You are 24 and there is a long runway.** Mentors invest in trajectory, not
  in finished product.

## 3. What he will probably ask

Have an answer ready for each of these. The starred ones are the ones people
fumble.

1. **"How many finished works do you have?"** — He wants the whole body, not
   the best five. Number matters: five is a taster, thirty is a practice.
2. ★ **"Do you work from photographs, from life, or from your head?"** — This is
   the most important question he will ask. The perspective distortions in your
   work read as invented, which is far more interesting than copied. Be
   completely honest either way, but know your answer and be specific.
3. ★ **"Why cities? Why never a person up close?"** — He will want a reason
   beyond "I like drawing buildings." You don't need a manifesto, but you need
   one true sentence.
4. **"How long does one take?"**
5. **"Where did you study?"** — Self-taught is a fine answer. Say it plainly,
   without apology. Do not soften it.
6. ★ **"Have you ever worked bigger? On canvas? In oil or acrylic?"** — Expect
   this, and expect it to become his main piece of advice. See §5.
7. **"How many could you make in a year?"** — He is asking whether you could
   supply a show.
8. **"Is this what you do, or what you do after work?"**
9. **"Have you sold anything? For how much?"**
10. ★ **"Where can I see this online?"** — Right now the honest answer is
    "nowhere": the Instagram is private and the Saatchi profile has two works
    and no bio. This is the one question you can turn from a weakness into a
    strength between now and tomorrow — send him the site.
11. ★ **"What do you want from me?"** — He will ask, in some form. A vague
    answer wastes the meeting. See §6.

## 4. What he can realistically do for you

Ranked by how much it is actually worth:

1. **Introductions.** To Yerevan galleries, to curators, to his own network.
   This is worth more than anything else and it is entirely within his power.
2. **A place in what he is building** — the residency, the emerging-artist
   gallery, the Yerevan hub. Being in that from the beginning is enormous.
3. **Honest critique from someone with nothing to sell you.** Ask for this
   first. It is the thing he is uniquely qualified to give you and the thing
   almost nobody asks for.
4. **Nomination or a route into the Creative Armenia–AGBU Fellowship**, which
   he has mentored for.
5. **Material and technical advice** — archival paper, lightfast media, working
   at scale, framing, and how to photograph work properly. Unglamorous, and it
   will save you years.
6. **Pricing.** Young artists almost always price wrong in one direction or the
   other. He knows what the market pays.
7. **Seeing a professional studio operate.** How a working artist organises a
   day, a body of work, a show.

**What he is not going to do, and must not be asked:** buy your work, lend you
money, become your agent, or promise you a show on the spot.

## 5. The one criticism to prepare for

Almost certainly he will raise **scale and materials.**

The work is small, on paper, largely in marker. Two consequences:

- **Most felt and alcohol markers are not lightfast** — the colours shift and
  fade in daylight over a few years. A gallery cannot responsibly sell an
  original that will not survive, and a serious collector will ask. If you
  already use pigment-based or archival pens, say the brand. If you don't,
  the honest answer is "I know, and I want to solve it" — that answer is
  respected; pretending it isn't a problem is not.
- **Small work has a low commercial ceiling.** Your pictures are dense enough
  to survive being three times bigger, and at that size they stop being
  drawings and start being paintings. Expect "have you tried this a metre
  wide?" and have a view on it.

Do not be defensive about either. Treat both as the interesting problem, not
as an attack. Your reaction to criticism is the thing he is actually measuring.

## 6. What to bring, and what to say

**Bring the originals.** The physical sheets, in a folder — not just a phone.
Your work is about density and it only fully lands in person, at reading
distance. This is the single most important item in this brief.

**Bring everything**, including earlier and unfinished pieces. Development is
what people look for in a 24-year-old. Editing yourself down to the five best
hides exactly what he wants to see.

**Bring the website on your phone** as a backup and as the thing to send after.

**Bring something to write with.** Take notes when he talks. It reads as
seriousness and you will not remember it otherwise.

**Do not bring a price list** unless he asks.

**Have two sentences ready.** One for "what are you doing?" and one for "what do
you want?" Something in the shape of:

> "I draw invented cities from memory, from several viewpoints at once, in ink
> and marker on paper."

> "I'd like your honest opinion on the work, and advice on what I should be
> doing next — bigger, different materials, or more of these."

That second sentence is deliberately not a request for a favour. Ask for
judgement, not for access. If he thinks the work is good, the access follows on
its own — and if you ask for access first, you have capped the meeting at what
you were bold enough to name.

## 7. Small things that matter

- **Do not apologise for the work.** Never call it a hobby, never open with
  what is wrong with a piece. Let him find the flaws; he will.
- **Do not ask for an introduction in the first ten minutes.**
- **Ask him about his own work.** He is moving away from realism into colour —
  ask him about that. It is a genuinely interesting question and it is what he
  is thinking about right now.
- **Speak in Armenian if that is easier for both of you.** Don't perform.
- **Follow up within 24 hours.** Short message, thank you, link to the site,
  nothing asked for. This is where most young artists lose the thread.
